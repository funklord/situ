# 26.672: The bills are dated, and the population is nine rather than 63

Status: recorded
Date: 2026-10-22
Source: `project.md` section 26

The copyright holder answered 26.671's question: date them. A date can
only mean "verified on", so applying the convention meant verifying every
claim first -- which is what narrowed the population and found a second
stale bill.

## The convention

A bill carries `// (verified YYYY-MM-DD)` on its own line at the end of
the paragraph making the claim. Nine lines carry one.

It is greppable, which is the whole of its lookup path:

    grep -rn 'verified 20' --include='*.situ' example/ std/

`verified 20` rather than the bare date, so it cannot be confused with a
decision citation like `(26.539)` or a source line like
`(initramfs.c:374)`. Six of the nine also anchor to a decision record, a
code site or a corpus location, and **an anchor is worth more than a
date** -- it says where to re-derive rather than only when somebody last
did. So the convention is: date, and anchor where you can.

**No gate.** The population is unfindable by grep -- a bill is confident
prose, which 26.671 established is indistinguishable from a sound one by
any mechanical test -- and an age-based nag over nine lines would
saturate the way `claude-guidelines` records a bare flag saturating.
The date is for a reader who is already looking.

## The criterion, which is the part worth keeping

26.671 counted 63 candidate lines and priced the convention at 63. The
real population is nine, and the difference is a criterion rather than a
sample:

**Date a claim that the language cannot express something. Do not date a
claim about what a construct does.**

The first is falsified *silently* by a language addition: the schema goes
on compiling, the map goes on being right, and nothing brings a reader
back to the sentence. The second is falsified *loudly* by a compiler
change, because every schema using the construct stops building.

So `example/ipv6ext:11` is not a bill, though it reads like one: "that is
a test on the header just read, which is what `while` asks and what
`until` cannot" describes two constructs that both exist. It cannot rot
the way a bill rots. The other 54 are format facts, which never go stale,
or deliberately past tense, like `example/sqlite`'s own "until
`[must_eq]` took a literal there was no way to say it".

## The second stale bill, and why the audit missed it

26.671 said one of 63 was stale. It was two, and the second is the
instructive one.

`example/smtp:68` said "what situ cannot do is *use* the separator to
decide how many lines there are". `while` (8.6.6) does exactly that, and
**the tail of the same file says so** -- it records the construct
arriving, names `example/ipv6ext` as where it landed, writes out the line
that would work, and leaves it out on purpose so the file stays the
record of a construct asked for and declined. The tail was updated and
the claim thirty lines above it was not.

That is `working-practice.md`'s rule about a claim living in more than one
place, with the correction landing where nobody re-reads: a reader of
`struct reply_line` reads line 68 and has no reason to reach the tail.
The header now says "could not, when this file was written" and points
forward; the tail is untouched, because rewriting it would erase the
example.

**The audit held the falsifier and traversed the link backwards.**
26.671's own list of bills it verified still true includes "`ipv6ext`'s
`while` versus `until`" -- `while` is the construct that falsified smtp,
and smtp's tail names ipv6ext by path. The pointer from the stale claim
to its own refutation was in the tree and was followed from the sound end.

So the transferable rule: **when the language gains a construct, the bills
to re-read are the ones that asked for it.** Not the corpus, not a sample
-- the files that recorded the request. Those are findable, because a bill
that asks for something names what it wants.
