# 26.914: a whole DNS message

Status: recorded
Date: 2026-11-06
Source: the lens 26.912 left, a limit written down and not tried

**`example/dns` stopped at twelve bytes and called the rest undescribed.** Its
own comment had corrected a wider claim once, and the narrower one was not tried
either. `example/dnsname` now states the message: four sections as long as the
header counts, and A, NS, CNAME, MX and AAAA as records, with any other type
carried by its length. No compiler change was needed.

An arm takes `rdlength` and holds its body, the shape `dnsrr` proved under
26.898, because `[holds]` belongs to a member of a struct and an arm has none.
That is five small structs, one per kind of body. It reads well enough for five
and is the argument for `[holds = T(args)]` if a format needs fifty.

**Measured against tshark.** The vector is assembled, since a local resolver
answers `localhost` with one record and this wants every arm: a CNAME, an A, an
AAAA, an MX, a TXT and four compression pointers. That it is assembled is why
tshark is the oracle: it decodes all 134 bytes with no expert complaint, and
`test_a_dns_message_reads_as_tshark_reads_it.py` holds situ to its types and
values. All four backends and both walkers read the vector through the corpus
tests. An ANCOUNT past the sections, an RDLENGTH past the message and an A
record of three bytes are all refused.

**Following a pointer stays a boundary.** `example/dnsname` argues that a view
is a base and a limit and that following a pointer is a resolver's security
decision, since nothing stops one pointing at another. 26.888 did not change
that: a constant offset from the message is not a chain of offsets the data
chooses. Whether `at` should reach one is a separate question and is not
answered here.
